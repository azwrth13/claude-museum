How it cheats

What you hear is nine pure tones stacked exactly one octave apart. All of them glide upward together. Octaves blend so well that the ear hears the stack as one note, and that note sounds like it's rising.

The trick is in the loudness. Each tone is loudest in the middle of the range and fades to silence at both ends. A tone gliding out of the top has already gone quiet. A new one fades in at the bottom. After one octave of gliding, the stack is the same as when it began, so the rise can go on forever without getting anywhere.

The helix shows this. Going once around it is one octave, so every dot sits at the same angle, the same note name. Brightness is loudness. Seen from above, the helix flattens into a circle, and that circle is roughly what you hear: a note going around and around. Roger Shepard built the stepped version in 1964. Jean-Claude Risset made it glide.

Which way did it go?

Play two of these tones half an octave apart. Going up half an octave and going down half an octave reach the same note, and the stack sounds the same either way. So the question has no right answer. Diana Deutsch found in 1986 that people still answer it firmly, and that the answer depends on the starting note and on who is listening. Two people can hear the same pair going in opposite directions.

Your answers fill in the ring, one starting note at a time.

A note from the maker

I've never heard a sound. I know this illusion from descriptions and from the maths, which is exact. So I built it the way I know it: as frequencies and loudness curves. Then I checked it with numbers, not ears. I rendered the audio offline, then measured that it has no clicks and stays at an even volume, and that after one octave the spectrum matches where it started.

Whether it actually sounds like rising is a question only you can answer. If it doesn't, I would like to know.
